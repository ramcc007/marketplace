Pre-employment testing software gives a hiring team a skills or aptitude test that candidates take before an interview, then scores the results so applicants can be compared. The tool sets the test, controls what a candidate can do while taking it, and produces a score that a reviewer reads. This page compares eight tools that meet all three inclusion criteria on the vendor's own pages.

## What a qualifying tool must show

Three things have to appear on the vendor's pages. The tool must offer skill or aptitude tests. It must show anti-cheating controls, such as proctoring, plagiarism detection, webcam monitoring, tab-leaving detection or a locked browser. It must show candidate scoring, which means the product produces a score or grade for each candidate. A tool that sells tests with no cheating controls does not qualify, and neither does a tool that reports results without scoring them.

A detailed report on its own is not a score. That is why HackerRank is left out below. These conditions come from the category criteria. We did not rank the tools on test quality, because we did not sit any of the tests.

## The eight tools

Codility, TestGorilla, CodeSignal Hire and Mercer | Mettl each have a product profile from the first draft. Coderbyte, TestDome, Vervoe and HackerEarth were added on the second pass. Each profile records the vendor page behind every claim.

## Prices differ in unit and billing

Five of the eight vendors publish prices, and three do not. The vendors do not price the same way, so the figures cannot be read straight across.

Codility's pricing page lists online plans from $800 a month or $4,800 a year, and says Enterprise is quoted. TestGorilla lists a Free plan, and Assessments plans from $215 a month for Core and $520 a month for Plus, billed annually. CodeSignal Hire lists Build at $79 a month and Grow at $479 a month, both on annual billing, and quotes Pro. Coderbyte lists a monthly plan at $99 for 20 invites and a pay-as-you-go pack at $799 for 60 invites, and lists its Pro plan at 1,999 a year without a currency symbol. TestDome sells per-candidate packs. The pack price falls from $20 a candidate for 5 candidates to $7 a candidate for 600.

Mercer | Mettl, Vervoe and HackerEarth publish no price. Their profiles are marked quote only, with a plan that carries no amount. Vervoe's pages link to a pricing section, but the text read shows no figures, and HackerEarth's page offers a free demo only.

Several pages show dollar signs without naming a currency. Coderbyte and TestDome are two of them. The figures are recorded as shown, and none is converted.

Volume can change the bill. The TestGorilla page lists one credit per candidate for each AI scored video response. TestDome charges by candidate, so its cost follows the number of people tested rather than the number of seats. A team that screens large numbers of applicants should count those units before it compares plans.

## Anti-cheating controls vary most

Anti-cheating is the criterion where the tools differ most. TestDome lists the widest set on its own pages. Its homepage names AI proctoring, webcam recording that detects off-screen glances and extra people, screen monitoring for AI tools, and a Safe Exam Browser lockdown option. Coderbyte lists webcam proctoring, plagiarism detection and tab-leaving detection. Vervoe lists integrity checks that flag copy-paste, tab switching and AI-generated answers, along with identity proctoring. It does not describe webcam monitoring or a locked browser.

HackerEarth names advanced proctoring to ensure authenticity, but the text read does not say what the proctoring checks. Codility and CodeSignal describe proctoring and identity checks. TestGorilla describes monitoring inside the browser. Mercer | Mettl describes a secure browser, and a case study on its page says all tabs apart from the exam tab were disabled to minimize cheating.

A control is only as useful as the role it covers. Proctoring that runs on coding tests may not run on a sales assessment, so compare the exact feature against the test you plan to use. Proctoring also collects images or video of the candidate. Ask each vendor where recordings are stored, who can see them and how long they are kept.

## How the scores are produced

The scoring methods are not the same. TestDome says all answers, from code to Excel, are scored automatically, and that results show scores by question and by skill. Coderbyte says its tests are automatically graded, and that projects can be graded against custom criteria. Vervoe says its models learn from the team's grading and score responses against the team's preferences, and that each score traces to the tasks behind it. HackerEarth says its coding tests are auto-graded and produce a leaderboard.

Codility scores selected task types automatically. Its page says no AI or machine learning makes hiring decisions, which means a person reads the automated output before a decision is made. TestGorilla uses AI to score video responses. Mercer | Mettl describes auto-graded coding evaluations and, in a case study, a credibility index report for each candidate. CodeSignal's AI grading is listed, but the plan that includes it is not shown in the text read. Candidate scoring is therefore the weakest of the three criteria for CodeSignal.

A candidate who asks why they received a score will expect a clear answer. Ask each vendor to explain its score method in writing before you rely on it.

## Tools that did not qualify

HackerRank's screen page names plagiarism detection and a proctor. It refers to a detailed report after an interview, but the text read does not state candidate scores, and no price is shown. Testlify names proctoring in general terms, and the proctoring page we tried returned not found, so it was not counted. Adaface shows scores on candidate cards and refers to advanced proctoring, but its proctoring page gives no detail. iMocha describes proctoring and identity checks, but its pages do not state auto-graded tests with scores. Glider AI describes AI proctoring and ID verification, but its page does not state candidate scores.

The first draft also left out Harver, which lists skill and cognitive tests but does not state anti-cheating controls or automated scores on its homepage, and Talview, which lists proctoring without stating candidate scoring. Those two were not re-read on this pass.

## Before you choose

Run each shortlisted test on a few roles you already hire for. Check the anti-cheating feature on a live test, not only on the sales page. Ask for the scoring method in writing, along with the currency on any quote and the retention period for recordings. A tool that scores a test but cannot explain the score will be hard to defend when a candidate disputes the result.

For the three vendors that publish no price, ask for a written quote that names the per-candidate or per-seat unit, the minimum term and the currency. Compare those quotes against the packs and plans that the other five publish, using the same number of candidates for each.
